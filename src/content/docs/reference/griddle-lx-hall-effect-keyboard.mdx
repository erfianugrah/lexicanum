GRIDDLE LX is a 5x12 ortholinear keyboard with Hall-effect (HE) switches: its own PCB (printed circuit board), FR4 (flame retardant 4) plate and case, on libhmk, an open-source HE firmware. This page is the hub of the series about it: why it exists, what each part is and why it beat the alternatives, and which page covers which part of the build. The name is GRIDDLE LX: a heavy flat plate with a grid on it, and LX for its 60 keys in Roman numerals (0x3C, the same 60 in hex, is the planned serial mark for the rev 2 board). The board files and firmware carry the same name.

<Aside type="note" title="Rev 1 is built and measured">
Rev 1 arrived and was brought up on 2026-11-20. High-Speed enumeration, the scan rate, mux settle, noise and press span are measured on rev 1 boards and labelled measured, in [Measured on rev 1](#measured-on-rev-1). Figures from before the board arrived stay in the page as computed or expected, labelled that way, next to what the board showed.
</Aside>

**TL;DR:**

- No commercial ortholinear HE keyboard exists (checked 2026-10-10 and 2026-10-26), and a 2026-11-03 survey of open HE PCBs found no 5x12 board either. The closest open ortholinear HE PCB is a 70-key board on a Full-Speed MCU (microcontroller unit).
- The stack is the one the fab-proven HE60 v2.1 uses: AT32F405RCT7 (on-chip USB 2.0 High-Speed PHY, which is what 8 kHz polling needs), libhmk firmware (QMK (Quantum Mechanical Keyboard) upstream has no HE feature set), 8x SN74LV4051A 8:1 muxes, 60x MT9102ET Hall sensors.
- Scan rate is 8 select steps x (mux settle + 8 ADC (analog-to-digital converter) conversions). Measured on rev 1, each step costs the settle time plus about 3.0 us, so the board scans at 24.9 kHz with a 2 us settle and 15.6 kHz at 5 us; the datasheet arithmetic had predicted at most ~11.4 kHz at 5 us. The settle sweep found no settling trend from 20 us down to 2 us, so the board ships with 2 us.
- Every part is on the bottom layer, so the board is assembled on one side; the switches have no electrical pins, so the build needs no soldering.
- The pre-fab review found the USB (Universal Serial Bus) data pair routed as two uncoupled traces on rev 1. Rev 1 enumerates at High-Speed anyway (480 Mbit/s, 125 us polling interval, measured); its signal quality is not measured. A custom DRC (design rule check) rule now flags the pair, and the gate holds rev 1's violations as a known baseline that fails on any new one.

## Why it exists

The author types on a 5x12 ortholinear board (a Boardsource Equals 60; its QMK keymaps are in [Creating QMK, VIA, and Vial keymaps](/guides/qmk-via-keymap-guide/)) and wanted the HE gaming features on that layout: a per-key actuation point, rapid trigger, per-key calibration and 8 kHz polling. Two searches came back empty:

- Commercial: no ortholinear HE or TMR (tunnel magnetoresistance) keyboard was on sale when checked on 2026-10-10 and re-checked on 2026-10-26.
- Open source: the 2026-11-03 GitHub survey found no 5x12 HE board. The closest open ortholinear HE PCB is KHEops70 (13x5+5 = 70 keys, STM32F303, GPL-3.0 (GNU General Public License), fab files published), then HE75 (75 keys in a GH60 outline, no licence file, and its README says "I haven't ordered the first set of boards yet").[^he75] Every other open HE PCB found is row-staggered, split or a macropad.[^kheops70]

The layout is new; the circuit blocks come from peppapighs' HE60 v2.1, a 60% staggered HE board released 2026-04-29 with its BOM (bill of materials) and pick-and-place files, on the same MCU, mux, sensor and firmware.[^he60] HE60 is GPL-3.0, GRIDDLE LX copies its symbols and footprints, and so the whole GRIDDLE LX project is GPL-3.0. The repo is private, though, so this is not an open-source project: the boards are for personal use, the design files and firmware are not published, and the file paths named in this series point into that private repo.

The GitHub backup mirror was deleted instead of force-pushed after a history cleanup because GitHub keeps Git LFS (Large File Storage) objects that are gone from history until the repository is deleted and recreated.[^gh-lfs-remove]

The sensors, the muxes and the MCU's analog supply (VDDA) share one LDO (low-dropout regulator) rail, as on HE60, so supply noise moves the sensor output and the ADC reference together. Each sensor sits on the bottom of the PCB directly under its switch's stem, where the switch's magnet is, and reads the field through the board.[^he60-mx1u]

## Which part, and why

### MCU: AT32F405RCT7

8 kHz polling means one HID (Human Interface Device) report per USB High-Speed microframe (125 us). Full-Speed USB (12 Mbps) caps polling at about 1 kHz, so the MCU needs a High-Speed (480 Mbps) port and, to stay single-chip, an on-chip High-Speed PHY (physical layer).

| MCU | USB | Open HE firmware | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| AT32F405RCT7 | High-Speed, on-chip PHY[^at32-ds] | libhmk (`at32f405xx` backend, 8 kHz) | picked |
| RP2040 / RP2350 | Full-Speed only (USB 1.1 controller and PHY)[^rp2040-ds][^rp2350-ds] | QMK forks and modules | 1 kHz ceiling |
| STM32F446 | no High-Speed PHY on chip | libhmk (`stm32f446xx`, Full-Speed) | 1 kHz ceiling |
| STM32F303 (KHEops70, HE75 lineage) | Full-Speed | QMK forks | 1 kHz ceiling |
| WCH (WinChipHead) CH32V307 | High-Speed, on-chip PHY | none; a libhmk port would be needed | fallback if AT32 stock runs out |

The AT32F405RCT7 is a 216 MHz part in LQFP-64 (low-profile quad flat package) and one 12-bit ADC with up to 16 external channels (GRIDDLE LX uses 8).[^at32-ds] Its High-Speed PHY takes its 480 MHz clock from a PLL (phase-locked loop) on the external 12 MHz crystal, and the datasheet rules out every other clock source, so the 12 MHz crystal is mandatory; `keyboard.json` carries it as `"hse_value": 12000000`. The CH32V307 row is there because it is what the Venom 60 HE class of commercial boards appears to use (from a teardown of the related Venom 87 HE, not the 60 itself), with TI (Texas Instruments) TMUX1308 8:1 muxes; it has the PHY and no open HE firmware.[^venom-teardown]

On 2026-11-03 JLC listed 209 AT32F405RCT7 in stock. Stock and prices can change, so check them at order time.

### Firmware: libhmk

QMK upstream has no HE feature set. The feature request "Standard functions for analog keyboards", opened 2025-12-13, drew a reply that nothing like it exists in QMK (from a contributor building HE support in a fork), and GitHub's stale bot closed it as not planned on 2026-05-26 with no maintainer decision.[^qmk-issue] The maintained open HE firmware with High-Speed USB support is libhmk (GPL-3.0, pinned at v1.8.1, commit `1d2b2e2`).[^libhmk] It is configured from a web app without recompiling, so the board definition is one `keyboard.json` and the keymap moves to the configurator.

libhmk's HID descriptor sets the polling interval to 1 microframe (8 kHz) when its high polling rate option is on, and to 4 (2^(4-1) = 8 microframes, 1 kHz) when it is off.[^libhmk-desc] 8 kHz has a cost on the host: the PC (personal computer) has to service the USB controller every 125 us, and [Fixing merged reports from an 8 kHz mouse on Windows](/guides/8khz-mouse-merged-reports-windows/) shows what a late DPC (deferred procedure call) does to that.

libhmk also smooths every key with an exponential moving average (alpha 1/16, overridable), updated once per main-loop pass. The lag it adds depends on the loop rate: on rev 1 with a 2 us settle and no USB host attached, the main loop measured 14,054 passes per second; the lag itself is not measured.

### Muxes: 8x SN74LV4051A, shared select

Eight 8:1 analog muxes give 64 positions for 60 keys; the 4 spare channels are tied to ground, as HE60 v2.1 does with its 3 (9 muxes, 72 positions, 69 keys). All eight share the three select lines (`MUX_SEL0..2` on PB5-PB7), and each mux output goes to its own ADC pin, so one select step presents 8 sensors at once.[^sn74lv4051a] The 2026-10-10 plan had four 16:1 CD74HC4067s, which need 16 select steps per scan against 8. All four boards in libhmk's `keyboards/` directory use parallel 8:1 muxes, and libhmk's scan code assumes that layout: shared select lines, one ADC channel per mux.

TI's TMUX1308 is a pin-compatible alternative (only pin 7 differs, ground vs not connected), cheaper on 2026-11-03 at JLC (\$0.2416 vs \$0.3442 at quantity 1).[^tmux1308] Its on-resistance is about 4 times higher: 120 ohm typical at 3.3 V against the SN74LV4051A's 30 ohm typical at 3 V (the LV4051A has no 3.3 V row). Both are far below the AT32 ADC's source-impedance limit at 7.5-cycle sampling.

### Sensors: 60x MT9102ET

The MT9102ET is a ratiometric linear Hall sensor in SOT-23 (small outline transistor): 1.600 mV/Gs typical at 3.3 V, 6.0 mA typical, with a range of only +/-850 Gs at 3.3 V.[^mt9102] HE60 v2.1 uses it 69 times. The range sets the switch choice: the Gateron KS-20 White and Orange are specified at 905 +/-80 Gs at bottom-out, above the sensor's +/-850 Gs range, so they were ruled out (a 1.6 mm board lowers the field at the sensor, by an amount nobody has measured). The Jade Ultra that was bought is specified at 122 Gs at rest and 550 Gs at bottom-out, over a 1.2 mm PCB, which leaves headroom under 850 Gs.[^jade-ultra] All switches on a board must have the same magnet polarity: polarity decides which way the sensor output moves, and libhmk's `invert_adc` is one setting for the whole board.

Sixty sensors at 6 mA is 360 mA on the analog LDO. The review put the TLV75733 in its SOT-23-5 package at about 0.6 W, with the junction near 150 C at 40 C ambient on the JEDEC (Joint Electron Device Engineering Council) thermal estimate. HE60 v2.1 runs the same part with 69 sensors (414 mA), and the 4-layer planes sink heat better than the JEDEC test board, so the regulator's temperature gets measured at bring-up before anything changes.

TMR was considered and not taken for rev 1. The TMR part that fits the same SOT-23-3 footprint, MDT (MultiDimension Technology)'s TMR2617S-AAC, draws under 300 uA instead of 6 mA and covers +/-1000 Gs, with its sensitivity programmed at the factory per order.[^tmr2617] The build JLC lists, 0.500 mV/V/Gs, works out to 1.65 mV/Gs at 3.3 V, within 3% of the MT9102ET, so it adds no resolution. The 1.000 mV/V/Gs build doubles the signal per gauss, but with the output limited to 5-95% of the supply it clips near +/-450 Gs, inside the Jade Ultra's bottom-out field. JLC listed none in stock on 2026-11-04 and again on 2026-11-07.[^tmr2617-jlc] What TMR would buy on this board is current: under 18 mA for 60 sensors against 360 mA, which would take the analog LDO out of the thermal question. It stays a rev 2 option, decided by the noise floor rev 1 measures at bring-up.

## Scan rate arithmetic

One full scan is 8 select steps. Each step waits for the muxes to settle, then converts the 8 ADC inputs in turn:

`scan time = 8 x (settle + 8 x conversion)`

One conversion at libhmk's 7.5-cycle sample time is 7.5 + 12.5 = 20 ADC cycles. libhmk divides the 216 MHz AHB (Advanced High-performance Bus) clock by 8, so the ADC runs at 27 MHz (the datasheet maximum is 28 MHz): one conversion takes 0.741 us, and the 8 conversions in a step take 5.93 us.[^at32-ds]

| Mux settle | Scan time | Scan rate | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 us (libhmk default) | 207.4 us | ~4.8 kHz | computed |
| 5 us (shipped, `"delay": 5` in `keyboard.json`) | 87.4 us | ~11.4 kHz | computed |
| 3 us | 71.4 us | ~14.0 kHz | computed; only if the muxes settle that fast |

All three rows are arithmetic on datasheet values from before the board existed. Rev 1 measured differently, in the other direction: with a scan counter in the DMA (direct memory access) interrupt, read over SWD (Serial Wire Debug) 10 s apart, each select step cost the settle time plus about 3.0 us, not plus 5.9 us, so the board scans faster than computed:

| Mux settle | Measured per step | Measured scan rate |
|---|---|---|
| 20 us | 22.99 us | 5,437 /s |
| 10 us | 12.99 us | 9,621 /s |
| 5 us | 8.03 us | 15,560 /s |
| 3 us | 6.03 us | 20,738 /s |
| 2 us (shipped) | 5.02 us | 24,888 /s |

The settle sweep held one key bottomed out and watched the next channel on the same mux. The worst neighbour moved -1.8 to -2.4 counts at every settle time from 20 us to 2 us, below twice its own noise; a shift that does not change with the settle time is not mux settling, most likely the pressed key's magnet seen by the neighbour's sensor. So the board ships with a 2 us settle. A sample is on average half a scan old, about 20 us at 24.9 kHz.

## Board: 4 layers, one side, no solder

| Layer | Carries |
|---|---|
| F.Cu | signals + ground fill |
| In1.Cu | +3V3A analog supply plane |
| In2.Cu | ground plane, next to the parts layer |
| B.Cu | every part, most routing, ground fill |

HE60 is a 2-layer board. Moving to 4 layers puts a continuous ground plane under the muxes, the MCU and the routing, with a copper-free window under each sensor, and a plane on the analog rail. The layer survey of open HE boards found that every fabricated one leaves a copper-free window over each sensor; GRIDDLE LX has 60 of them, with no vias and no fill, on F.Cu, In1.Cu and In2.Cu.

Every part is on B.Cu because JLC's Economic PCBA (printed circuit board assembly) assembles one side only, and double-sided assembly forces the Standard service with higher setup and stencil costs. The switches go on top and need nothing electrical from the board: the KS-20 has no leads to solder, only plastic pegs and a magnet on the stem axis. Gateron lists the Jade Ultra in the KS-20 family (SKU (stock keeping unit) KS-20GFW10B045NW-Y106);[^jade-ultra] no Ultra drawing was found, so the footprint follows the KS-20 White drawing, two holes of 1.70 +/-0.05 mm,[^ks20] which HE60's footprint drills at 1.75 mm.[^he60-mx1u] The switches clip into the FR4 plate, and the plate is screwed to the PCB with M2x10 screws through 3.4 mm spacers, 5.0 mm from plate top to PCB top as the MX figure asks. On an HE board, flex between the plate and the sensors reads as key travel, so any softness belongs between that plate-PCB pair and the case. With the PCBA from the fab, the assembly is screws, switches and keycaps, with no soldering iron.

The order, including the 4-layer form settings that keep Economic PCBA available, is in [Ordering a 4-layer bottom-side PCBA and FR4 plate at JLC](/guides/jlc-4-layer-pcba-keyboard-order/).

## What the pre-fab review found

Five automated reviewer agents ran in parallel (electrical, PCB and fab, firmware, mechanical, tooling and docs) after rev 1 was ordered, and a judge agent checked the evidence behind each design-affecting finding. One of the 17 findings bears on the 8 kHz goal:

| Finding | Evidence | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| The USB data pair between the ESD (electrostatic discharge) part and the MCU is routed as two separate, uncoupled traces | sampling every 0.1 mm of D+: 9.8 mm sits 5 mm or more from D-, 16.2 mm at 2-5 mm, only 1.2 mm within 0.5 mm; HE60 runs its pair within 1 mm for 70 mm | estimated 100-116 ohm differential against the 90 ohm +/-15 % target; the run is about 30 mm, so it may still enumerate at High-Speed. Outcome on 2026-11-20: rev 1 enumerates at 480 Mbit/s; signal quality not measured |

The reviewer called the routing tolerable for a Full-Speed link; the judge raised it to a rev 1 risk because the link is High-Speed, the reason the board uses an AT32F405. The autorouter does not couple the pair, so the rev 2 fix is in the rules: the board's custom DRC rules now require the pair to be coupled (gap 0.10-0.25 mm, at most 5 mm uncoupled, at most 1.0 mm skew). Rev 1 breaks all three (gap up to 5.1 mm, 32 mm uncoupled, 2.9 mm skew); the gate holds those exact violations as a known rev 1 baseline that a rev 2 change must remove, and any other hit fails it. How the checks are built, and how each one is shown to fail on a known-bad board, is in [A PCB as code, and proving its checks](/reference/pcb-as-code-verification/).

Two other findings affected the case: the milled cavity's inside corners collided with the PCB's square corners and with a square brass weight, and the case checks had passed both. Those are in [A keyboard case in build123d with DFM (design for manufacturability) checks](/guides/keyboard-case-build123d-dfm/).

## Artery's AN0290 reference design

Artery publishes its own AT32F405 magnetic-keyboard demo: a 68-key board with 8 kHz polling and a 16 kHz scan, with application note AN0290 v2.0.0 (2026-01-22) and a 9-page schematic.[^artery-an0290] The repository has no PCB layout and no licence file, so its ideas can be read but its files cannot be copied. It was found after rev 1 was ordered, so nothing in it changed rev 1; it is the second data point for rev 2.

| Block | Artery AN0290 | GRIDDLE LX rev 1 (HE60-derived) |
|---|---|---|
| Hall sensor | HX49ESO x68 | MT9102ET x60 |
| Sensor and mux supply | 4.65 V from a buck-boost then an adjustable LDO, ferrite on its input | +3V3A from a TLV75733 on VBUS |
| Analog mux | ADG708 x9 on the 4.65 V rail | SN74LV4051A x8 on +3V3A |
| ADC inputs | series resistor + unpopulated capacitor footprint per input | direct |
| Reference check | 2.5 V reference into an ADC channel to track supply drift | none |
| Select lines | PC13-PC15 | PB5-PB7 |
| Filtering | EMA (exponential moving average) plus two Kalman filters | EMA (libhmk) |

A higher sensor supply gives a larger output span per gauss, and the mux's on-resistance is one term in the settle time the scan-rate table depends on. Both designs need the same two things for the PHY: the 12 MHz crystal and a 12k resistor on `OTGHS1_R`.

## Measured on rev 1

All on rev 1 boards between 2026-11-20 and 2026-11-23, with the board's own ADC read over USB or SWD; no oscilloscope was used.

| Item | Result | How |
|---|---|---|
| USB link | 480 Mbit/s, polling interval 125 us on the vendor and keyboard endpoints | sysfs `speed` and endpoint `interval` on Linux, libhmk build |
| 8 kHz reports | 7,998.5 reports/s (479,913 in 60.0 s) | a test build of the in-house firmware that only sends vendor reports |
| Scan rate | 24,888 scans/s at 2 us settle, 15,560 at 5 us | scan counter in the DMA interrupt, libhmk build |
| Mux settle | no settling trend from 20 us to 2 us; worst neighbour -1.8 to -2.4 counts at every setting | one key held, its neighbours watched, 1,000 reads each |
| Rest noise, no switches | 1.21-2.44 counts standard deviation (median 1.62), 7-13 counts peak-to-peak, none railed | 400 raw reads per key, filter off |
| Rest noise, fully assembled | 14 counts peak-to-peak median, 16 max, rest 2143-2218 counts | 65,535 raw samples per key, all 60 switches in the plate |
| Full-press span | 445-500 counts above rest (median 475) | the maximum reading while each key was pressed once, all 60 keys |

The span tells whether the sensor saturates (derived): at 3.3 V the MT9102ET sits at half the supply, about 2,048 counts, with no field, and at 1.6 mV/Gs its +850 Gs limit is about 1,360 mV higher, about 3,735 counts. A full press peaks around 2,690 counts, so the Jade Ultra on a 1.6 mm board uses roughly 38% of the sensor's range above zero field and does not saturate.

Not measured yet: the analog LDO's temperature under the 360 mA sensor load, rest drift over temperature, signal quality on the USB pair, and key-to-wire latency (the method is in [Measuring keyboard latency on High-Speed USB](/reference/keyboard-latency-measurement/)).

## Which do I pick

```dot

  rankdir=TB; bgcolor="transparent"; nodesep=0.3; ranksep=0.5;

  fork [label="Start from that board"];
  blocks [label="New layout from\nHE60 v2.1 blocks"];

  q1 -> hs [label="yes"];
  q1 -> fs [label="no"];
  hs -> q2;
  q2 -> at [label="yes"];
  q2 -> ch [label="no, or no AT32 stock"];
  at -> q3;
  q3 -> fork [label="yes"];
  q3 -> blocks [label="no (5x12 ortho)"];

```

1. If you need 8 kHz polling, you need an MCU with an on-chip USB High-Speed PHY. If 1 kHz is enough, a Full-Speed MCU works: STM32F446 with libhmk, or an RP2040 with a QMK fork.
2. With a High-Speed PHY, AT32F405 with libhmk is the combination with maintained open firmware. CH32V307 has the PHY but needs a libhmk port; it is the fallback if AT32 stock runs out.
3. If an open HE board already exists in your layout, start from it. If not (a 5x12 ortholinear has none), build the layout new from the HE60 v2.1 blocks on the same stack.
