## Per-Video Rules

### Video 1: DesignCourse - Visual Hierarchy in UI Design

- **Weight is the first lever for hierarchy.** When two elements of same size sit next to each other, making one bold immediately establishes which is the heading and which is the body. [~02:49] "making this bold immediately improves the design overall."
- **Size separation must be unambiguous.** In a three-element stack (subheading, title, body): the title must be largest; the subheading must be "noticeably different than the description text but not too big to where it's competing with the title." [~07:16] If a subheading approaches the title's size, "now they're kind of too similar."
- **Multiple separation levers are additive.** Apply size, weight, colour, letter-spacing, and background containers together. Each lever reinforces the others. [throughout]
- **Extended tracking on small labels.** On a subheading label, extended letter-spacing + bold + small size creates a distinct "category label" level. [~09:12] "a little bit of extended tracking, which is letter spacing, and we could also just get this color."
- **Equal padding inside label containers.** "when it comes to putting text in containers like this or buttons like this, you want to make sure you have equal white space all around." [~10:08]
- **Bold compensates for lower contrast.** "if you make something bold, you can actually afford to make it less contrast, or if it's really large." [~15:01]
- **Underline = clickable.** "people will universally recognize it as something they can click for sure so you can't go wrong with underlines." [~14:05]
- **No single right answer; pick one and be consistent.** Demonstrated 3-5 acceptable variants per example. [throughout] - **opinion, not justified with data**

### Video 2: Hierarchy in Design - Typography, Scale & Layout

- **Every size in a hierarchy must be unique.** If two elements share the same size, the reader cannot decide which to read first. [~00:44] "these are same size, which one to choose? Same way audience who are looking at it will think which one to read."
- **Scale = attention.** "As humans, we pay attention for the bigger things." The largest element always wins. [~01:17]
- **Hierarchy needs balance and white space.** "When it comes to layout, you need other design principles help. If you wanna fix hierarchy in your layout, you need balance and white space." [~02:04]
- **White space is non-negotiable.** "White space is most important. If you're not giving proper breathing space between the elements, then the design won't be completed." [~02:21]
- **Grids for alignment.** "The fastest way to do it is using grids." [~02:27]

### Video 3: Satori Graphics - Advanced Hierarchy

- **The Three Flow Rule.** Ask three questions per design: (1) Where do eyes land first? (2) Where second? (3) Where do they finish? [~00:08] - **heuristic, not empirical**
- **F-pattern and Z-pattern are design scaffolds, not laws.** Most western readers scan top-left -> right -> down-left (F) or diagonally down (Z). Use these to place hierarchy levels. [~01:18]
- **The Three-Level Rule for sizing.** 1. Hook (largest) - catches from afar. 2. Secondary Detail (second largest). 3. Finisher (smallest) - crucial info + CTAs. [~02:54]
- **Hook != most important.** The largest element can be a product image or visual hook; the critical info may be at the finisher level. [~03:34]
- **Grouping centre trick.** One larger object centred in a group of equals creates automatic hierarchy. [~04:16]
- **White space enables hierarchy.** "Hierarchy literally cannot survive or work without white space. The space allows distinctions to be made by the viewer so hierarchy can actually be identified subconsciously." [~05:51]

### Video 4: 15 Rules to Adjust Typography Like a Pro

- **Line height scales with line length.** "The longer the paragraph, the tighter the line height and vice versa." [~00:55] Shorter lines = less line-height. Quote said "tighter" for longer but then contradicted himself explaining the opposite - in practice he showed longer text getting MORE line-height. **opinion, self-contradictory**
- **Line length: 13-15 words per line.** "every line should fit around 30 to 50 words per line" then corrected to 13-15. [~02:23] - **opinion** (30-50 was an error)
- **Letter spacing vs size: tighter for larger.** 16px = 0%, 48px = -2%, 96px = -4%. Below 16px add +1-2%. [~03:46] - **opinion with specific numbers**
- **Letter spacing vs weight: tighter for bolder.** Thin = -2%, regular = -4%, inherently bold face = 0%. [~04:34] "the chunkier the characters, the less space you will want to have between them." - **opinion**
- **Default kerning by design intent.** Display faces designed for large sizes; text faces for 12-16px. Don't over-adjust: "Typefaces have adjusted letter spacings for the default sizes the typeface is supposed to be used in." [~06:04] - **fact about type design**
- **No overlapping characters.** "when you overlap the characters they lose legibility." [~06:46]
- **Line-height must account for ascender/descender clashes** when content is dynamic. With fixed content, tighter is OK. [~07:46] - **practical constraint**
- **Gestalt proximity: vertical space > horizontal space.** If line-height drops below letter-spacing, characters group vertically instead of horizontally. [~09:29]
- **Smooth rags.** "A good rug is a rug that's smooth in its curves." Avoid deep valleys/peaks in ragged-right text. Manual line breaks OK for titles (3-5 words). [~10:52]
- **Don't force rectangular text blocks.** When mixing sizes, keep the natural rag. "The more contrast you have when changing hierarchies, the better." [~11:59]
- **No widows or orphans.** [~14:29]
- **Never stretch/shrink type.** [~15:14]
- **Correct characters.** Use curly quotes, proper × symbol, etc. [~15:28]
- **Book recommendation:** Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style*. [~16:25] - **cited authority**

### Video 5: No BS Guide to Typography

- **3-5 font sizes for a whole project.** "I choose between 3-5 font sizes for the whole project, not to make many styles." [~04:55] - **opinion**
- **Size steps must be big.** "The steps between font sizes should be big. The difference will help a lot with contrast and create visual hierarchy." [~05:02] - **opinion**
- **Use a type scale.** Recommends typescale.com. [~02:20] - **practical advice**
- **Leading: body 120-150%, headings 85-100%.** [~02:32] - **opinion with specific numbers**
- **Letter spacing: body -1% to 0%, headings -3% to -5%.** [~02:52] - **opinion**
- **Pick fonts with many weights.** "choose one with a lot of different thickness so you have more flexibility." [~02:04]
- **Alignment: left-aligned 90% of the time.** "by default it's very easy to read since there are no inconsistencies at the start of each line." [~07:10]
- **Center alignment: max 2-3 lines.** For headings: 2-3 lines of very few words. For body: max 3 lines. [~07:37]
- **Right alignment: rarely, only for very short text or big headings.** [~07:54]
- **A11Y contrast checker** - always test colour contrast. [~04:20]

### Video 6: Line Length for Web Design

- **50-70 characters per line is the sweet spot.** [~01:47, ~02:28] "The key takeaway isn't just about restricting text to a narrow width. It's about maintaining 50 to 70 characters per line for readability."
- **Too short (<15 chars):** disrupts readability, "feels awkward, unnatural." [~01:13]
- **Too long (>140 chars):** "forces the reader's eyes to travel too far reducing readability." [~00:38]
- **The number, not the pixel width, matters.** "Even with a wider layout, you can achieve this balance by adjusting the font size accordingly." [~01:47]
- **Practical example:** 18px font at 800px width = 50-70 chars. A 1300px container should limit text to an 800px column. [~02:06]
- **Headings exempt from char count.** "There's no strict minimum requirement of characters per line because heading can be as short as five characters." [~02:54]

## Rules That Apply to a Dense Docs Page (with wide tables and diagrams)

| Rule | Why it applies | Priority |
|------|---------------|----------|
| **50-70 chars/line for body prose** | The single most actionable finding. Current 34rem at 18px overshoots 50-70. Constrain content column, not the whole page. | HIGH |
| **Headings exempt from char-count and line-length constraints** | Headings are short by nature; don't squeeze them into a narrow prose column if they look wrong. | HIGH |
| **Big size steps between hierarchy levels** | 32px h1, 26px h2, 21px h3 = small delta (1.23x). Should be at least 1.5x-2x between levels per v5. | HIGH |
| **At least two properties per level distinction** | Current: all headings are Overpass Mono caps. Only colour + size differ. Add weight or tracking contrast between h1/h2/h3. | MED |
| **Heading line-height: 85-100%** | Currently likely too loose. Check if h1/h2 use default line-height. | MED |
| **Prose line-height: 120-150%** | Current 1.75 (=175%) is above this range. Could tighten. | MED |
| **Three levels of hierarchy across the page** | Hook (h1 title) → secondary (h2/h3, first paragraphs) → finisher (references, link graph). Tables and diagrams should fit into secondary or finisher without violating the reading flow. | MED |
| **Left-align everything body-related** | Already done; confirm center-alignment only for short headings (≤2 lines). | LOW |
| **White space between sections > within sections** | Gestalt proximity rule. Check if whitespace between h2+h3+body blocks visually groups them. | MED |

## Rules That Only Fit Posters / Landing Pages

| Rule | Why it doesn't translate |
|------|--------------------------|
| Z-pattern / F-pattern as primary layout | Docs pages are scrolled, not scanned in a single viewport. The pattern applies to the above-fold but the page extends far below. |
| Hook as an image or visual element | Docs pages have no hero images. The hook is the page title. |
| Extended tracking on small labels for "premium" feel | Decorative; adds no function in technical docs. |
| Background colour blocks for heading containers | Distracting in long-form reading. Use borders/bars instead (already done with left bar). |
| Smooth rags in titles | Docs titles are single-line; the rule is irrelevant. |
| 3-5 font sizes for the whole project | Docs sites have body, h1-h3, code, references, sidebar, TOC - easily 8+ distinct levels. The constraint is too tight. |
| Center-alignment for visual balance | Never center body prose in technical docs - hurts readability. |

## Opinion vs Justified

**Opinion (designer preference with no data cited):**
- Exact % values for letter-spacing (-2%, -4%, -5%)
- "3-5 font sizes" cap
- "big steps between sizes" (no ratio given)
- "smooth rags" (aesthetic preference)
- DesignCourse's "you have options" framing
- Extended tracking for "premium" feel

**Justified with reasoning (logical, not empirical):**
- 50-70 chars/line: based on eye-travel distance and re-finding next line
- Bold compensates for lower contrast: "chunkier characters" need less space to stay readable as words (v4)
- Default kerning by design intent: type designers already built spacing for intended sizes (v4)
- Ascender/descender clashes: objective collision problem (v4)
- Gestalt proximity: vertical space must exceed horizontal space or lines group wrong (v4)

**Cited authority (not verified in this research):**
- Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style* (v4) - typesetting bible
- David Carson: "we often mistake legibility with communication" (v4)
